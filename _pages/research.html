Overview

For the past five years I have built benchmarks and evaluation methods for AI models in social NLP applications, working closely with doctors, students and other domain experts across healthcare and education. Those collaborations kept exposing the same gap: technologists and real-world stakeholders often disagree about what a "good" output is, and AI built from a single viewpoint carries real risks once it is deployed.

That gap shapes my research philosophy. I want to make AI models inclusive and safe for widespread societal adoption across many perspectives and domains. During a PhD, I want to pursue that by developing rigorous theoretical and practical approaches for robust AI benchmarking and evaluation in multi-perspective and changing social contexts.

The lens: construct validity

While writing my Master's thesis, I borrowed construct validity from the social sciences to describe my work in one general framework, drawing on Jacobs & Wallach [1], Wallach et al. [2] and Bean et al. [3]. Construct validity asks how well an unobservable construct is captured by observable proxies. In human-centered benchmarking, the proxies are our datasets and evaluation metrics. The constructs are defined by social values and norms.

Most social NLP benchmarks and evaluation frameworks currently measure a singular, fixed construct, usually aggregated or majority-driven. In practice, social constructs are often justifiably pluralistic and contextually dynamic. As AI models become part of everyday life, benchmarks and evaluation should account for this, and we need formal frameworks and methods for doing so [4, 5].

Direction 1 · Q1

Formalizing benchmarking and evaluation for pluralistic social contexts

Standard benchmarking frameworks assume a convergent ground truth and treat annotator disagreement as noise, which can push under-represented viewpoints aside. Designing benchmarks and evaluation for pluralistic constructs is complex, underexplored, and an active challenge for the NLP community. Recent work that applies game theory [10] and psychometrics [11] to related NLP problems suggests that these neighboring perspectives can help formalize the problem.

Approach A: stakeholders as players in a contextual game

Inspired by work on contextual games for multi-agent learning [12], I would model communities or stakeholder groups as players whose preferences depend on socially contextual rules. A model is then evaluated not by agreement with one label but by its contextual regret with respect to each group.

Illustrative formulation

Rg(f) = 𝔼c ∼ 𝒟g [ maxa ug(a, c) − ug(f(x, c), c) ]

For each stakeholder group g, the regret is the expected gap between the best achievable utility in its contexts and the utility of the model's actual output. This is a sketch of the idea, not a finished definition.

Approach B: group-specific constructs with item response theory

A more practical route is to segment data points by how culturally differentiable they are and to develop a separate evaluation construct for each group, using psychometric tools such as Item Response Theory (IRT) [13]. The same item can be easy for one population and hard for another. IRT makes that difference measurable instead of averaging it away.

Direction 2 · Q2

Synthetic benchmarks and evaluation under social context drift

My work on synthetic clinical dialogue introduced me to multi-agent social simulations as a playground for modeling and evaluating agents under social context drift. Simulations make agent behavior and environment variables explicit and controllable, and recent work shows they can model realistic social settings [14, 15].

The open problem is deciding which social factors matter enough to model mechanistically when depicting drift into an unseen environment. There is no established way to estimate those factors when relevant social studies do not exist, or to formally decide which evaluation metrics remain valid there.

My proposed approach uses causal analysis. I would model the simulation as a structural causal model over social factors and construct-related outcomes, and use causal discovery on limited real data to identify which factors actually affect evaluation. Context drift then becomes a change in specific causal mechanisms, and transportability theory [16] can state the conditions under which metric scores carry over to a new context.

Approach: borrowing formal tools from neighboring fields

The papers that have influenced me most solve seemingly complex problems with an elegant abstraction borrowed from another domain. I want my doctoral research to work the same way: human-centered NLP problems, made precise with formal machinery from adjacent fields. I have been studying these fields through courses and the related literature to develop these ideas.

Game theory Stakeholders as players with context-dependent utilities. Models are judged by contextual regret, not majority agreement.
Psychometrics Item response theory and construct validity to separate group-specific constructs and quantify item difficulty per population.
Causal inference Structural causal models, causal discovery and transportability to reason about evaluation under context drift.

Both questions sit at the boundary between human-centered NLP and formal machine learning, and I don't think either can be answered well from one side alone. I bring depth in human-centered evaluation and domain-expert collaboration. I am looking for an environment that adds formal rigor in causality, psychometrics and game theory.
