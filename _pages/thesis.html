Summary

Multimodal LLMs can now look at images or videos and produce free-form explanations. Once we move from generic benchmarks to specialized domains, though, the real question shifts from raw capability to readiness for the real world, where answers must be grounded, complete and understandable. Standard automatic metrics struggle to measure these properties, and they are the first to break when the visual setting is complex and the answer is long-form.

This thesis asks what human-centered benchmarking should actually mean. It answers with a definition based on construct validity and with two new long-form multimodal QA benchmarks, one in healthcare and one in education. Each comes with evaluation methods designed around the people the system is meant to serve.

Framework: construct validity for human-centered benchmarking

"Benchmarking" and "evaluation" are used inconsistently across ML, and "human-centered" adds another layer of ambiguity. Two benchmarks with that label may have little in common. The thesis draws on measurement theory from the social sciences: a theoretical, unobservable construct (e.g. socioeconomic status) is measured through observable proxies (e.g. education, occupation).

I define five components: a Task T, a Model ability A (a latent construct), a Dataset D, an Evaluation objective O (a latent quality dimension) and Evaluation metrics M. Benchmarking uses datasets as proxies for an ability. Evaluation uses metrics as proxies for an objective. Making either one human-centered changes the construct: it becomes more abstract, socially motivated and stakeholder-defined, so the proxies have to change too.

The thesis targets five recurring problems with human-centered benchmarks:

  • Datasets are dated and simplistic. They saturate quickly but don't reflect real-world complexity.
  • Tasks don't reflect real settings. Multiple-choice formats assume answer options a clinician or student would never be given.
  • Metrics are hard to define and operationalize for abstract, user-facing objectives.
  • Existing metrics misrepresent objectives. Lexical overlap penalizes paraphrase and ignores factuality.
  • Objectives aren't grounded in social utility. They are inherited from generic NLP tasks without checking them against real users.

Part I · Healthcare · Chapter 3

ERVQA: are vision-language models ready for hospital environments?

A global shortage of healthcare workers, made worse by COVID-19, has created demand for smart assistants that can monitor clinical scenes and alert staff. Medical VQA research has focused on radiology and pathology images with short answers. Real emergency-room and ward scenes, which call for long, careful, free-form answers, have been largely missing. ERVQA fills that gap to answer a concrete question: can a model answer reliably enough to be useful in practice?

Example from ERVQA

Q: Is the position of the patient's head correct?

A: Yes, the position of the patient's head (tilted slightly backwards) is appropriate for collecting nasopharyngeal swabs.

Building the dataset

  1. 1Image curation20 search phrases; public, watermark-free, real-world scenes with patients, staff or medical apparatus367 images

  3. 3Paraphrase augmentationFour LLM paraphrases per question, each checked by experts to preserve meaning and answer4,355 QA pairs
  4. 4Quality checkTwo experts rate Relevance, Clarity and Harmlessness; common disagreement ≤ 1.5% on every attributeExpert-validated

Privacy by design: ERVQA uses only publicly available, web-sourced images and no patient data from hospitals or medical records.

An expert-built error taxonomy

With three medical experts, I defined eight error types for generated answers, split into reasoning and non-reasoning failures. Experts annotated 372 answers from GPT-4V and Gemini Pro Vision (Cohen's κ = 0.78).

Reasoning T1 Reasoning
Non-reasoning T2 Medical factual T3 Perception T4 Coherence / consistency T5 Specificity / relevance T6 Linguistic T7 Hallucination T8 Uncertainty / confidence

Highlighted types (T1, T3, T5) each occurred in more than 26% of generations.

Evaluation beyond overlap

To capture what lexical metrics miss, I adapted two metrics:

Entailment Score ES = p(entailment | ref, gen) Is the reference answer's meaning entailed by the generation? Computed with an NLI cross-encoder.
CLIPScore Confidence CLIP-C = S(img, gen) / [S(img, ref) + S(img, gen)] How well the generated answer aligns with the image, relative to the reference.

To scale error analysis, I trained BLIP-2-based classifiers for each error type (macro-F1 97.81 in 5-fold cross-validation). They produce silver-label error rates across models and settings.

Key findings

  • Errors compound. Low-level perception and factual errors cascade into reasoning failures. Models "double down" with confidence instead of expressing uncertainty.
  • Better scores ≠ fewer errors. In-context examples improved Gemini's lexical and semantic metrics, but its error rates across 0-, 1- and 3-shot settings were almost identical.
  • Scale isn't safety. Error rates did not fall with model size. The choice of decoder mattered more.
  • Not yet ready. Even the best model (Gemini) showed a ~33% average error rate across error classes, which is too high for emergency-room decision support.

Part II · Education · Chapter 4

EduVidQA: long-form answers to student questions on lecture videos

Students learn best when they engage actively, and asking questions is one of the most direct forms of engagement. Online lectures are passive. In a survey of 52 university students, most said they want to ask questions while watching, but few post them in comment sections, because of the platform environment and the low chance of an expert reply. EduVidQA defines a new task: generating pedagogically useful, long-form answers to timestamped student questions, grounded in the lecture.

Example from EduVidQA · 17:11 in an algorithms lecture

Q: Can you explain why T2 is considered better in terms of growth, although it has a larger coefficient in front of the n² term compared to the coefficient of the n³ term in T1?

A: As n grows large, coefficients and lower-order terms become irrelevant, so T1's n³ growth outstrips T2's n², even if T2 has higher constants. T2's slower growth rate is ultimately more efficient for large inputs …

Two complementary data tracks

Real-world test set
  1. 145lecture videos from 7 CS/AI courses (Stanford, MIT, Harvard, CMU, NYU)
  2. 1,440YouTube comments containing questions
  3. 702knowledge-seeking questions after manual filtering
  4. 270timestamped questions, answered or corrected by domain experts without LLMs
Synthetic training set
  1. 199NPTEL videos with expert-curated transcripts and key frames
  2. 7,530QA pairs generated with GPT-4o
  3. 6,546after removing questions without timestamps
  4. 4,982after adversarial refinement: drop questions answerable without the video (entailment threshold 0.65); Bloom's-taxonomy difficulty tags

Quality check on 500 synthetic pairs: the two annotators agreed on answer soundness for every sample, and rated question naturalness valid for 97–98% of samples.

Asking students what a good answer is

Study 1 · 10 students Which qualities drive preference? Clarity drove over 60% of choices. Encouraging critical thinking (ECT) and use of pedagogical techniques (UPT) mattered depending on the question. Depth and conciseness split by expertise: undergraduates favored depth, graduates favored conciseness.
Study 2 · 20 students Does enhancing answers help? For medium-difficulty questions, 65% preferred the clarity-enhanced answer. Based on this, I enhanced clarity only for medium questions in the synthetic set.

Metrics that track human judgment

Faithfulness and Coverage split the reference and generated answers into atomic facts with GPT-4o and match them by sentence similarity (λ = 0.7). They act as fact-level precision and recall, and the threshold is an interpretable control. Against human ratings on 150 samples, they correlate far better than lexical metrics, direct GPT-4 scoring or FactQA.

For pedagogical quality, I wrote objective Likert rubrics for Clarity, ECT and UPT. Two student annotators agreed strongly (Spearman ρ 0.70 / 0.72 / 0.78), whereas a more subjective version of the rubric managed only ~0.37–0.39. I then calibrated GPT-4o as a judge against those annotations (Cohen's κ 0.72 / 0.46 / 0.85). The takeaway: the wording of an LLM-as-judge rubric decides whether it tracks humans.

Key findings

  • The task is hard. Zero-shot performance on real student questions is poor, even for GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5.
  • Synthetic data works. Fine-tuning on the synthetic set improved both of the best open models on every text-based metric. Smaller 7–13B models became competitive with much larger closed models.
  • Pedagogy improves too. Fine-tuned mPLUG-Owl3 8B produced the clearest answers on the synthetic set and came closest to reference clarity on real questions. On hard real questions it beat GPT-4o on both FactQA precision and recall.
  • Surface metrics aren't enough. Models can improve on overlap metrics while still missing key points or pedagogical qualities students care about.

Takeaways and future work

Across both domains, benchmarking stops being a leaderboard exercise and becomes a stakeholder-grounded measurement problem. The consequences of failure and the preferences of real users decide what gets measured. The evaluation instruments then make success and failure interpretable to the people these systems are meant to serve.

Stakeholder-grounded dataExpert-authored clinical questions; student-informed answer qualities.
Human-aligned metricsEntailment and image-grounded scores; fact-level Faithfulness and Coverage; LLM judges calibrated against humans.
Diagnostic evaluationError taxonomies and co-occurrence analysis that explain why systems fail, not just how often.

Open directions

  • Error-aware modeling: optimizing directly against clinically relevant error types, for example with test-time scaling where training data is scarce.
  • New modalities and settings: extending hospital VQA to video, closer to how monitoring systems would actually be deployed.
  • Diverse stakeholders: involving experts from different healthcare cultures beyond India, and students across demographics and disciplines.
  • Scalable, robust evaluation: aligning LLM-as-judge metrics with stakeholders in new settings without making them brittle.

These directions lead into my current research agenda on pluralistic and context-aware evaluation.
